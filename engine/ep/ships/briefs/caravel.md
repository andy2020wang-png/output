# Brief: caravel

## Style card
15th-century Portuguese portolan chart on vellum, kept COOL: grey-toned vellum, sepia/brown-black ink coastlines with the characteristic dense names-perpendicular-to-coast style (use generic tiny strokes, not readable fake names), rhumb lines from 16-point roses in black, verdigris green and vermilion, small flag-free ornaments. No gold leaf dominance (shot 7 is golden). Avoid inventing readable place names; if any text, keep it generic/decorative and unreadable-small.

## The one shot
A slow pan along the chart; rhumb lines draw themselves one by one from the central rose; a small caravel (lateen-rigged, two or three masts with triangular sails, low hull, no square sails) sails a zigzag upwind course — tacking on beats 5 and 13 (sail swings across and fills). Show the wind direction (a soft wind arrow or a wind-head ornament in the chart idiom) blowing against the course, so the zigzag reads as beating to windward (place it on a northbound homeward leg off West Africa against the NE trades).

## Text
opener(): 卡拉维尔帆船 in a vermilion/black chart-lettering idiom (Cinzel/IM Fell + Noto Serif SC); subs() on a calm vellum area at the bottom.

## Next transition (for awareness; built later in a separate phase)
Out: the compass rose rotates (≤ 60°, eased) and blooms into gunsmoke.
Keep the last beat (local 9.375-10 s; hook 14.375-15; outro holds to the end) compositionally stable with that anchor element clearly visible.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Show a Portuguese caravela latina, not a galleon and not Columbus's ship. It is small (about 20–25 m; Britannica gives about 23 m) with a shallow draught (about 1.7–2 m), a low freeboard and a length-to-beam ratio of about 3.5:1. It should look nimble, not towering.
- Low bow with NO forecastle. The only raised deck is a single sterncastle/poop at the stern. A high forecastle belongs to the nao Santa María and to carracks.
- Smooth, flush carvel planking with a dark tarred or pitch-brown hull. No overlapping clinker planks (that is Viking), no painted gunport rows, no copper sheathing, no portholes, no figurehead.
- Steering: a rudder hung on the sternpost and worked by a tiller. No ship's wheel (that is 18th century) and no side steering oar.
- Rig: 2 or 3 pole masts, each with ONE triangular lateen sail on a long, steeply sloping yard. The front end of the yard sits low near the bow and the back end rides high. The mainmast yard is roughly as long as the hull. The largest sail is forward, the smaller mizzen aft, and the masts rake slightly forward. No jibs or staysails, no square topsails, no crow's-nest platforms.
- Sails are off-white or cream canvas. A red Cross of the Order of Christ (a red cross pattée with a thin white inner cross) on the sails is the right emblem for Portuguese exploration ships. Use it ONLY on Portuguese ships.
- Flags to AVOID: the modern Portuguese green-red flag (1911), the modern Spanish red-yellow flag (1785), the Burgundy saltire (16th-c. Habsburg), the Union Jack or any British ensign, the Dutch tricolour, the Jolly Roger. Acceptable: the Order of Christ cross, the 15th-c. Portuguese royal arms (white field, five blue quinas shields, red border with gold castles). For Columbus's fleet only: a white banner with a green cross and crowned green F and Y.
- If Columbus's ships appear at all, Niña and Pinta after the Canaries refit are caravelas redondas (square sails on the fore and main masts, lateen on the mizzen), and the Santa María is a bulkier nao with a high forecastle and square sails. Do not draw three identical lateen ships.
- Zigzag geometry: each leg should be about 55–65° off the wind, so successive legs are about 110–130° apart. Never show the ship pointing almost straight into the wind. The ship heels slightly to leeward and the sail bellies to the lee side.
- At each turn the lateen yard and sail swing over to the other side of the mast (real crews usually wore round or dipped the yard). Time this swing to land on the beat.
- Wind logic: off NW Africa the trade winds blow from the NE, so a southbound ship there runs downwind and does not need to zigzag. The upwind zigzag is accurate either on the homeward (northbound) leg off NW Africa or southbound below the equator against the SE trades and the Benguela current (Diogo Cão / Dias, 1480s). Show the wind's direction (for example a labelled wind on the rose) so the zigzag reads correctly.
- Crew: about 20 men in 15th-c. dress (wool tunics, hose, hooded cloaks or caps). No tricorns, no striped sailor shirts, no ranked navy uniforms. No oar banks (do not confuse it with a galley).
- Chart: ink and colour on vellum/parchment, with networks of rhumb lines radiating from 16- or 32-point wind roses. No latitude/longitude grid and no Mercator look. If the shot is set before 1492, do not draw the Americas. Draw the Cape of Good Hope only if the pan reaches 1488 (Dias). Treat the conventions as typical rather than universal: place names written perpendicular to the coast on the land side, major ports in red. The north mark varies (star or T on early charts, a fleur-de-lis on later ones).
- The ship should appear as a living illuminated miniature (in the manner of ships drawn on Portuguese charts) large enough to read, about 1/4 of the frame height or more, rather than a tiny icon on the route line.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
The Renaissance portolan chart is era-appropriate and fits the ship. Portuguese charts are exactly contemporary: Pedro Reinel c.1485 (the oldest signed Portuguese chart, reaching the Congo) and Jorge de Aguiar, Lisbon 1492 (the oldest signed and dated one). Visually it stands well apart from the cobalt 青花 before it, and the glaze-to-parchment transition is good. The single shot (pan along the chart, rhumb lines drawn one by one, ship zigzagging upwind) is legible.

Risks and fixes:
(1) Wind logic. A southward pan off NW Africa is downwind under the NE trades, so put the zigzag either below the equator (against the SE trades, 1480s) or on the homeward northbound leg. Show the wind direction.
(2) Palette clash with shot 7. Parchment beige and gold can blur into the Turner gold smoke that follows. Keep this shot cooler: sepia ink, verdigris green, vermilion, a slightly grey vellum. Let Turner own the saturated gold.
(3) The no-small-text rule. Real portolans are covered in tiny place names. Replace them with illegible calligraphic strokes, or use only one or two large, deliberate labels, so nothing reads as small corner text.
(4) Subtitle legibility on a light background. Use a soft dark vignette or band at the subtitle zone and dark text with a light halo, or white text on a translucent dark plate.
(5) Compression on Douyin. Hairline rhumb lines alias and shimmer, so draw them at 2 px or more in slow strokes, limited to 16–32 lines from one or two roses.
(6) Scale. Make the ship an illuminated miniature that is clearly a ship, with the lateen yard flipping at each tack, rather than a dot on a route.
(7) The compass-rose-to-cannon-smoke transition is a strong idea. Keep the rose's spin slow and eased so it does not strobe.
