# Brief: steam

## Style card
Hand-tinted 1830s lithograph (like the Sarony prints of the Great Western): crayon grain anchored to objects (not to the screen), a buff tint stone with white highlights scraped out, Prussian-blue wash on the sea, black hull with a thin painted band, warm grey smoke as open crayon texture thinning as it trails. Hatching spacing ≥ 3 px at 1080p, anti-aliased, to avoid moiré.

## The one shot
A parallel tracking shot alongside an 1838-type transatlantic paddle steamer moving left→right: wooden hull, one tall funnel, side paddle-wheel housing amidships, four masts with some fore-and-aft sail set (auxiliary). The paddle wheel turns exactly once per 八拍 (5 s), a float entering the water on every beat; an engine "heave" (subtle hull/smoke pulse) on beats 1, 5, 9, 13. In the background, a heeled sailing packet (square-rigged) falls behind — the card is about beating sail time.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Hero ship: make it the Great Western of 1838, to match the card. Do not draw a generic Mississippi sternwheeler or a later liner.
- Hull: a wooden oak sailing-ship hull with iron straps, long and fairly low, with a classic bow and a square or transom stern. About 65–72 m long; draw it as a large wooden ship, not a blocky iron liner. Use no rivet lines, no steel plating and no plimsoll or load-line mark (1876).
- Propulsion: one large side paddle wheel on each side, amidships, enclosed in half-round paddle boxes. No screw propeller (first transatlantic screw ship was 1845) and no stern wheel.
- Rig: four masts carrying auxiliary sails. The 1838 RMG lithograph shows her 'travelling under steam only, her sails furled and flags flying from all four masts, her bow and her stern', with the ship's name on the mizzen pennant. Furled sails are the strongest visual statement of 'not depending on wind'.
- Funnel: draw one tall funnel amidships between the paddle boxes, as period prints generally show; check against the 1838 lithograph. Do not use Cunard red-and-black bands (Cunard began in 1840) or two or more funnels.
- Smoke: it streams aft and downwind of the funnel, never forward. Fulton and the 1838 voyages ran into headwinds, so a straight-aft smoke plume suits the story.
- Background: the RMG print shows 'sailing vessels leaning in the background'. Use heeled, close-hauled merchant packet ships (three-masted, square-rigged), not ships of the line, which belong to the previous chapter.
- Flags: a British merchant red ensign at the stern, with the post-1801 Union (including the St Patrick saltire) in the canton. Avoid the White Ensign, modern house flags and any 'Blue Riband' pennant; that term was coined later.
- If the Clermont of 1807 appears at all (for example as a ghosted engraving): a long, narrow, flat-bottomed boat about 146 x 12 ft, with an exposed engine, open unboxed paddle wheels at the trial, one tall thin smoke pipe and two masts. Its US flag is 15 stars and 15 stripes (1795–1818), not 50 stars.
- Crew and passengers, 1838: sailors in short blue jackets and round hats or caps; officers in dark frock coats; passengers in top hats, tailcoats, bonnets and shawls. No life jackets, electric lights or radio masts.
- Paddle speed: the Great Western's engines ran at about 12–15 rpm. At 96 BPM one wheel revolution per 八拍 (5 s, 12 rpm) is historically plausible and syncs to the grid. Put engine-stroke accents every 2 beats rather than spinning the wheel fast.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
Era fit is good. Victoria came to the throne on 20 June 1837, so the Great Western's April 1838 voyage falls in the Victorian era (the 1807 Clermont does not, so keep the hero ship the 1838 one). Prints of the Great Western made at the time were mostly hand-coloured lithographs (Sarony/Robinson 1838, RMG PAH8859). The planned engraving look, with hatching lines and watercolour washes on cream paper, is close enough and reads more clearly on screen. It also stands apart from the neighbouring chapters: Turner-style warm oil impasto before it and silver-grey monochrome tintype after it.

Risks:
(1) Moiré and shimmer of fine hatching while the camera tracks. Keep hatch spacing at 3 px or more at 1080p, anti-alias the lines, and anchor the hatching to the objects rather than to the screen, to avoid a 'shower-door' effect.
(2) It could look too much like the gold-on-black line drawing of the opening. Use sepia or brown-black ink on warm paper with restrained washes: Prussian-blue sea, buff sky, black hull.
(3) Dense smoke can read as ink blots. Draw it with open cross-hatching that thins as it trails off.

Single-shot judgment: a parallel tracking shot with the paddles turning on the beat is right and continuous. I suggest three upgrades. First, keep left-to-right screen direction, consistent with the canoe, so 'progress' reads the same way. Second, have the steamer, sails furled, pass a heeled sailing packet in the background, quoting the 1838 RMG lithograph; this shows the 'half the time' card without any text. Third, set the wheel at one revolution per 八拍. The planned transition (developing like a negative into silver-grey) fits the ironclad's tintype well.
