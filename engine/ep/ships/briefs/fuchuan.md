# Brief: fuchuan

## Style card
Blue-and-white porcelain (青花瓷), painterly: underglaze cobalt with soft bleed, varying wash density, heaped dark cobalt spotting (Yuan/early Ming "heaped and piled"), white glaze with a gentle curved specular sheen and very slight crackle; the plate rim carries a 海水纹 (stylised wave) border. No ruled lines, dimension marks or blueprint feel.

## The one shot
Starts low-key and lights up over the first 2 beats (glaze sheen sweeping). Top-down/three-quarter on a plate showing a Fu ship (broad flat transom stern, high stern castle, battened lug sails — two or three masts, pick one consistent type), then a slow push-in that turns into a cutaway of the hull showing the transverse bulkheads (隔舱板) dividing it into watertight compartments (draw 13 compartments readable). On beat 5 (2.5 s, as the card appears) ONE compartment breaches and floods with heaped dark cobalt that rises and settles by beat 12; the other compartments stay bare white; the ship stays afloat (waterline unchanged or barely lower). Creak at beat 14.

## Text
opener(): 中国福船 in cobalt calligraphic-weight serif (or Ma Shan Zheng) on the white glaze; subs() with cobalt ink on a white glaze band.

## Next transition (for awareness; built later in a separate phase)
Out: the plate rim becomes the 16-point circle of a portolan rhumb-line network; its centre becomes the compass rose.
Keep the last beat (local 9.375-10 s; hook 14.375-15; outro holds to the end) compositionally stable with that anchor element clearly visible.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Cutaway must show exactly 12 transverse bulkheads making 13 compartments, to match the card. Space them unevenly, roughly 1.5 to 2 m apart and closer together toward the ends. Each bulkhead is a stack of thick horizontal planks laid edge to edge across the V-section.
- Hull section is a deep V with a pointed bottom (底尖上阔) on a heavy keel. It is not flat-bottomed, since the flat bottom belongs to the northern 沙船. The surviving Quanzhou hull is about 24 m long and 9 m wide, a long-to-wide ratio near 3:1, so the ship looks beamy and stout, not slim.
- Bow and stern both rise high (首昂尾高). The bow is flared, with a small transom bow board. The stern is a broad, high, square transom with a multi-tier stern castle (明史: 柁楼三重 on the 大福船). Paint 'dragon eyes' (龙目, oculi) on both sides of the bow, a hallmark of Fujian ships.
- Steering is a large central axial rudder hung at the stern, which can be raised and lowered. Do not draw a side steering oar or a Western pintle-and-gudgeon rudder on a sternpost.
- Rig: two masts for a Ming 大福船 (明史: 帆桅二), or three for the Song merchant ship. The foremast rakes forward. Sails are battened lug sails (硬帆) of matting or cloth with full-width bamboo battens and fan-shaped multi-part sheets (缭绳). No square sails on yards, no jib or bowsprit, no ratlines or crow's nest, no Western standing rigging.
- Lowest hold carries ballast stones or earth (明史: 最下实土石). Mast steps (桅座) sit on the keel. For cargo in the Quanzhou ship's holds, show bundles of incense wood or sacks, not barrels.
- Flood physics: the breach is a small hole below the waterline. Water fills only that compartment and stops level with the sea outside, not up to the deck. The hull then settles slightly and trims a little toward that end. Water never crosses a bulkhead, and the other compartments stay dry and white.
- Crew, if shown: Song or Ming working clothes, meaning short jackets, trousers, head-cloths or topknots, and conical bamboo hats. No Qing queues (辫子), which only began in 1644.
- Flags to AVOID: the Qing yellow dragon flag (1889), any PRC or ROC flag, large single-character TV-drama banners (帅/明), and the Japanese hinomaru. Plain unlettered pennants or streamers are safest.
- Other anachronisms to avoid: Zheng He nine-mast 'treasure ship' reconstructions, rows of gun-ports or portholes, glass windows, metal rivets and plating, and Western frame-and-rib hull drawings (the junk's transverse strength comes from its bulkheads). Avoid VOC or Dutch ship motifs and the English 'Willow pattern' (1790s) on the porcelain.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
Blue-and-white porcelain (青花瓷) is a strong, readable choice. It looks clearly different from the cold aurora before it and the parchment portolan chart after it, and the glaze-to-parchment transition works. It also fits the history: blue-and-white matured at Jingdezhen in the Yuan and Ming and was shipped out in bulk through Fujian ports (Quanzhou, and Zhangzhou ware), the Fu ship's own trade. One caveat is that strict Song fidelity would call for celadon (青瓷), because blue-and-white came about 50 to 100 years after the Quanzhou ship. With the era line '宋代起', this stylisation is acceptable.

Risks and fixes:
1) Monochrome readability. If the flooded compartment uses the same cobalt as the line work, it will not read as water. Fill it with a deeper, heaped cobalt wash (Yuan or early-Ming 'heaped and piled' dark spotting) and leave the dry compartments as bare white glaze, so the contrast tells the story. Add a thin outer waterline in a lighter wash.
2) Single continuous shot. Start with the camera looking straight down at the plate, with the ship painted in profile. Push in until the painted hull 'opens' into a cutaway, but keep the camera axis fixed so the move reads as one push-in, not a cut. Use a sea-wave border (海水纹) or lotus-scroll border (缠枝莲) on the rim for period flavour.
3) Timing. Start the breach and flooding as the card appears (about 2.5 s), so viewers read '一舱破损进水' while it happens. Let the water stop at the waterline and the hull settle by about beat 12, then hold steady. The 'ship does not sink' moment should land on a strong beat.
4) Show the 13 compartments clearly; count them on screen if possible.
5) No text on the plate. That avoids small corner text and the cliché of a 福 character.
