# Brief: nile

## Style card
Middle Kingdom painted wooden tomb models (like the boats from the tomb of Meketre, c. 1980 BC, Met Museum / Egyptian Museum Cairo): carved wood covered in white gesso and flat paint — red-brown skin for figures, green or black hull paint with yellow/red bands, unbleached linen sail, painted eyes. Show it as a model "come to life" on a stylised painted river (flat blue with black zigzag water lines, the Egyptian convention). Light: soft museum-like key light from upper left; slight wood grain and paint chipping. NOT an ochre plaster wall, NOT a relief, no block-border friezes, no winged sun (episode 1 used those).

## The one shot
Low lateral dolly along the sailing model: square linen sail on a single mast with yard and boom, steering oar at the stern, crew figures. The sail is slack at first and FILLS on beat 9 (5.0 s) — the north wind, sailing south (upstream). Behind it, a rowing model with its mast lowered (lying on supports) passes in the opposite direction, rowers pulling (northbound with the current). The direction contrast IS the card's fact. Rasterised low-poly look is fine (WebGL triangles / canvas polygons with baked gesso textures); avoid raymarching (budget).

## Text
opener(): 古埃及帆船 in a clean area; subs(): consider a band like a painted register strip (flat ochre/blue edge) or a soft dark band.

## Next transition (for awareness; built later in a separate phase)
Out: the painted river's black zigzag lines flood outwards into the glossy black slip ground of a red-figure vase.
Keep the last beat (local 9.375-10 s; hook 14.375-15; outro holds to the end) compositionally stable with that anchor element clearly visible.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Most important rule: the ship under sail travels SOUTH, against the current. Make the carved zigzag water ripple gently one way (north) while the ship and its billowing sail move the other way. The sail bellies toward the direction of travel, with the wind behind it.
- If a second boat is shown, it goes NORTH: mast lowered and stowed on a crutch or stanchion, crew rowing, no sail. This matches the Met's Meketre pair and the Ure Museum Beni Hasan pair.
- Show the two hieroglyphs large, as part of the carved relief and not as corner text. P2 is a boat with one mast, a square sail and a steering oar ('south'). P1 is a boat with no mast or sail ('north'). Signs face the same way as the figures, toward the start of the line.
- Hull: long, shallow, crescent or spoon-shaped, with high upturned stem and stern, often ending in papyrus-umbel finials. No keel, no visible ribs, no deep V-hull. A long Old Kingdom ship or a New Kingdom seagoing ship may carry a rope hogging truss running bow to stern over forked props.
- Choose ONE period and do not mix rigs. Old Kingdom (c. 2600–2200 BC): two-legged bipod (A-frame) mast with a tall, fairly narrow square sail. Middle–New Kingdom (c. 2000–1100 BC): single pole mast with a very broad, low sail set between a top yard and a bottom boom, held by many lifts from the masthead (as in Hatshepsut's Punt reliefs, c. 1470 BC). The Middle–New Kingdom type is the most iconic.
- Steering: Old Kingdom ships use several large steering oars at the stern quarters, worked by standing helmsmen. Middle and New Kingdom ships use one or two big steering oars on a stern post with a tiller, as on the Meketre models. Never show a hinged sternpost rudder.
- Crew: composite-profile Egyptian figures (head in profile, eye and shoulders frontal), white linen kilts, bare chests, short dark hair. Men are painted red-brown ochre, the conventional skin colour. Add a bow lookout with a sounding pole and a helmsman at the stern. When under sail, the rowers sit idle.
- Deck: a cabin or canopy amidships or aft, sometimes with chequered, shield-like painted panels. Sail of off-white linen; New Kingdom paintings occasionally show patterned sails.
- Palette is limited to Egyptian pigments: Egyptian blue, malachite green, red ochre, yellow ochre, carbon black and gypsum white on a limestone ground. Avoid purple, neon or saturated modern hues. Water is drawn as blue/blue-green bands of vertical zigzags, optionally with fish and papyrus or lotus clumps.
- AVOID: triangular or lateen sails. Modern Nile feluccas have them, and they are the most common anachronism here. Also avoid multiple masts, jibs, crow's nests, Greek bow eyes or bronze rams, oar banks in tiers, and any flags or pennants (Egyptian ships flew no national flags). Avoid the modern Egyptian flag, Arabic script, camels, and Greek or Roman columns. If pyramids appear, use them only as distant west-bank shapes and keep them out of a Theban scene.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
The style is right for the era and clearly different from its neighbours. Shot 1 is monochrome, cool misty silhouettes; shot 3 is black-figure pottery in black on orange; this one is polychrome painted limestone relief with depth lit by moving light. Shots 2 and 3 are both flat, profile-view ancient art, so the relief must feel three-dimensional to stay distinct: carved edges lit by a moving light, normal-mapped incisions, soft cast shadows.

One historical wrinkle: Egyptians mostly used sunk relief on sunlit exterior temple walls, and painted raised relief inside tombs. Two fixes work:
- Better: keep the sunk relief but replace torchlight with low golden-hour sunlight sweeping across the wall. It is accurate, warm and gives a cleaner light sweep.
- Or: keep the torch and make it painted raised relief in a tomb chapel. Keep any flicker slow and low in amplitude, with no strobing.

How to make the single shot carry the card:
- Make the lateral pan follow the ship's direction of travel (south).
- Have the carved water ripple the opposite way.
- Let the light reach the ship just as its sail bellies out.
- During the card's 7 s, frame the P1 and P2 hieroglyphs (boat without sail, boat with sail) carved large beside the ship, so 'the hieroglyph for south is a ship under sail' is seen as well as read. An optional small north-bound rowing boat with its mast down in an upper register gives the contrast without a cut.

The planned transition (stone curling into a pottery surface, tone turning terracotta) leads well into the Greek vase.
