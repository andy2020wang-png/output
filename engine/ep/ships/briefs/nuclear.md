# Brief: nuclear

## Style card
Contemporary drone/satellite photography at blue hour: deck, ocean and island built from textured planes in perspective plus a few boxes (rasterised, NOT raymarched); the ocean is one plane with a procedural normal map, Fresnel and sky reflection; deck lights and the island's lights glowing softly. Clean, modern, slightly desaturated grade. No hull numbers, flags or text.

## The one shot
Start at a high oblique over the angled flight deck (folded-wing jets parked, small crew figures in coloured jerseys for scale), then rise and tilt to straight down; the FULL REVEAL of the whole ship lands exactly on beat 9 (5.0 s, music lands in E major). Kelvin wake opening at its true 19.47° half-angle behind the ship. Keep the ship in the upper 60 % of the frame after 2.5 s so the card is clean. A distant jet passes at beat 7.

## Text
opener(): 核动力航母 in Inter Display / Noto Sans SC 700, white; subs() over the dark sea at the bottom.

## Next transition (for awareness; built later in a separate phase)
Out: the top-down V-wake becomes the canoe's paddle ripples as dawn returns.
Keep the last beat (local 9.375-10 s; hook 14.375-15; outro holds to the end) compositionally stable with that anchor element clearly visible.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Commit to one class: a Nimitz-class silhouette (10 ships, 1975 to 2009) matches the card. Do not mix it with Ford-class features (smaller island set farther aft, three elevators, flat-panel radar faces) or with Enterprise CVN-65's 1960s square island with SCANFAR panels and conical dome.
- Proportions: about 333 m long; flight deck about 77 m wide; waterline beam only about 41 m. The flight deck overhangs the hull heavily, especially the large port sponson under the angled deck.
- Flight deck: dark grey non-skid surface. Flat bow with no ski-jump. Angled landing deck about 9 degrees to port, outlined in white with a broken white centreline. Four catapults (two at the bow, two in the waist on the angled deck) with yellow lead-in lines and raised jet-blast deflectors behind them. Red-and-white foul line.
- Island: one island only, on the starboard side, roughly amidships (Nimitz). It has a tall mast with rotating radars, a bridge, and the flight-control position at its aft top. There is no funnel and no smoke: nuclear carriers have no stacks. This is the key difference from the steam and WWII chapters.
- Elevators (Nimitz): four deck-edge elevators. Three are on the starboard side (two forward of the island, one aft of it) and one is at the port quarter.
- Hull colours: US Navy haze grey (light blue-grey) sides, a black boot-topping band at the waterline, and dark red below it. The hull number is white and appears on the island and on the flight deck. Do not paint large numbers on the bow sides. Show anchors in hawsepipes forward and hangar-bay openings at the elevators.
- Wake: four propellers make a broad, churned white centre wake inside a long Kelvin V. Speed is 30+ knots, with a modest bow wave because the hull is long and fine.
- Aircraft (contemporary): F/A-18E/F, EA-18G, F-35C, E-2D (twin turboprop with rotodome), MH-60 helicopters, CMV-22B. Park them along the deck edges with wings folded. Avoid the F-14 (retired 2006), WWII propeller fighters, Harrier or F-35B (STOVL types), and J-15/Su-33 (ski-jump carriers).
- Night lighting must be sparse and dim: white and amber flush dots along the landing-area edges and centreline; a vertical row of drop lights down the stern; the amber 'meatball' landing lens on the port side with green datum lights; red (port) and green (starboard) sidelights; white masthead light; dim red light in the island. Avoid stadium floodlights, sweeping searchlights and neon.
- Flags: the safest choice is to omit them. If one is shown, use only a small, correct US ensign on the island mast while under way (the bow jack is flown only in port). Never show PLAN, Russian, Royal Navy or Rising Sun flags, or an invented flag.
- Do not show the reactors: no glowing core, no blue Cherenkov light through the hull, no radiation trefoil, no mushroom-cloud imagery. The naval reactors are sealed deep inside and nothing on the outside shows the ship is nuclear.
- Escorts are optional. If used, show distant modern destroyer silhouettes (Arleigh Burke class, with a funnel). Do not show nuclear cruisers, which were all retired by 1999.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
The style is right for the era and distinct from its neighbours. "当代电影感夜海（实时海面渲染）" is the film's only photoreal style, which suits the present day. It contrasts with chapter 11's WWII gouache poster, and continuing from 11's "天色入夜，甲板灯亮起" works well. The single shot (deck level, pulling back to a high three-quarter aerial that shows the whole ship and wake) is a good climax.

Risks and fixes:
- **Full night will look murky.** A grey ship on a black sea will crush to mud and band badly after Douyin's H.264 compression. Use blue hour or moonlit twilight instead: a lighter cobalt horizon band, a moon or sky path on the water, and rim light on the island and deck edge. Add film-grain dithering.
- **A procedural photoreal carrier can look like a cheap game asset.** Use a clean, simplified hard-surface model and let atmosphere carry the realism: haze, depth fog, wake foam and sparse lights.
- **The scale change is large for 10 s.** Ease the move in and out, show crew-sized cues (folded-wing jets, small figures) at the start, and keep the ship in the upper 60% of the frame from about 2.5 s on so the bottom subtitle card never covers it. Land the full reveal on the downbeat of the second 八拍 to match the musical peak.
- **Set up the transition.** In the last two beats, lift the horizon toward pre-dawn so "天色回到黎明" and the wake-to-paddle-ripple transition feel continuous. A near-top-down final angle makes the V-wake easier to morph into the canoe's ripples.

Sounds to avoid: arresting-wire clank, jet whine and afterburner crackle, catapult shuttle slam, boatswain's pipe, ship's horn, PA voices, alarms or klaxons, sonar pings, radar beeps, and the Geiger-counter cliché.
