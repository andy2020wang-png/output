# Brief: trireme

## Style card
Attic RED-FIGURE pottery (dominant in 5th-century Athens): glossy black slip ground, figures and ship left in reserved terracotta orange, internal details in fine black relief lines and diluted-glaze brown lines, a meander/ovolo border band above and below the frieze. Specular highlight on the curved glaze. Do NOT use black-figure (that is 6th century).

## The one shot
Locked camera, the vase rotates slowly so the frieze scrolls past (curvature visible at the edges: compress near the silhouette, highlight band moving). The trireme in profile: long low hull, bronze ram at the waterline, eye on the bow, curving stern ornament (aphlaston), three tiers of oars visible as three rows of oar blades; ALL oars stroke together on beats 1,3,…,15 (one stroke every 2 beats). Stylised waves in the frieze below.

## Text
opener(): 希腊桨帆战船 in a clean black glaze area (terracotta-coloured lettering suits); subs(): bottom band in black glaze with terracotta text or the default ink.

## Next transition (for awareness; built later in a separate phase)
Out: the black glaze wave on the vase swells to fill the frame and becomes the carved night sea.
Keep the last beat (local 9.375-10 s; hook 14.375-15; outro holds to the end) compositionally stable with that anchor element clearly visible.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Hull proportions: long, low and narrow. The Olympias replica is 36.9 m long and 5.5 m wide (the Hellenic Navy gives 33–43 m by 3.5–4.4 m for ancient ships), with a draft of about 1 m and very low freeboard. It must look like a long, slim dart, not a tall galleon.
- Oars: three staggered tiers, about 31 + 27 + 27 per side, one man per oar, all of similar length. The top (thranite) tier pivots on an outrigger that sticks out past the hull. Middle oars come over the gunwale and the lowest (thalamite) oars come through small ports near the waterline. Never draw the tiers as stacked vertical columns, and never put several men on one long sweep. That arrangement belongs to later polyremes and Renaissance galleys.
- Stroke timing: all three tiers move together, and blades enter the water together. Olympias reached about 38–45 strokes per minute in a sprint and 30 at cruising pace. At 96 BPM, one stroke every two beats (48 spm) looks right for ramming speed. Eighth-note strokes, as in the plan, would mean 192 spm, which is frantic and unrealistic.
- Bow: bronze ram at the waterline, a greenish-bronze casting with three horizontal blades. The Olympias copy follows a ram in the Piraeus museum; Hellenistic rams such as Athlit show the type. Add a smaller secondary ram (proembolion) higher up, catheads (epotides) projecting on each side of the bow, and a painted eye (ophthalmos) on the bow. Marble ship eyes have been found at the Zea naval harbour and the Agora.
- Stern: the hull sweeps up and curls forward into a fan-shaped aphlaston. Steering is by two quarter-steering oars, one on each side. There is no stern rudder.
- Rig: one mast amidships with a single square sail, plus a small boat-sail forward, used only when cruising. In battle the masts were lowered and the sails were often left ashore. If the shot depicts battle or ramming, show no sail. No lateen or triangular sails.
- Crew visible: the Lenormant Relief shows the top-tier rowers working nude. On the light deck there are only about 10 hoplites (round shield, crested helmet) and 4 archers, plus a steersman at the stern. No castles, towers, catapults or the Roman corvus.
- Colour, if the ship is rendered realistically: a dark pitch-black hull (Homer's 'black ships'), red ochre near the bow, bronze ram, painted eye. In red-figure vase style it becomes terracotta-orange figures on glossy black, with fine black relief lines and diluted-glaze brown for hatching. Use added white or purple-red sparingly.
- Avoid: chained galley slaves and whip-wielding overseers (the Ben-Hur trope; Athenian rowers were mostly paid free men), national flags of any kind (no modern blue-and-white Greek flag, no Roman SPQR or eagle, no Byzantine double-headed eagle), Spartan lambda shields on an Athenian ship, Viking-style shield rows along the hull, dragon or animal-head prows, cannons, and a stern rudder.
- Vase conventions: strict profile view, no perspective or cast shadows, sea shown by a wave band (running-spiral or 'Laconian wave') or dolphins, a meander (Greek key) band above the frieze. Base the ship's anatomy on the Lenormant Relief and Olympias, because no surviving vase clearly shows three tiers of oars. Drawing a trireme on a vase is artistic licence, which is fine as stylization.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
Switch the style card from 古希腊黑绘陶器 to 雅典红绘陶器 (Attic red-figure). The Met's Heilbrunn essay dates the invention of red-figure to about 530 BC, probably in the Andokides workshop, and it then gradually replaced black-figure. By the Salamis era (480 BC) and through the 5th century, red-figure was the dominant Athenian fine-ware style; black-figure lived on mainly in conservative uses such as Panathenaic amphorae. Ships do appear on vases in both techniques, but differently. The best-known ship vases are Archaic black-figure, for example the Exekias Dionysos cup of about 530 BC (Munich 2044), so black-figure fits the 6th century, not Salamis. Red-figure ships from the Salamis generation exist but are rarer. Examples are the Siren Painter's stamnos with Odysseus' ship under oars (British Museum 1843,1103.31, c. 480–470 BC), the Brygos Painter lekythos of Athena holding a trireme's aphlaston (Met 25.189.1, c. 480–470 BC), and a red-figure cup fragment with a ship (Vienna University 503.48, 475–425 BC). The only clear 5th-century image of trireme rowing tiers is the marble Lenormant Relief (Acropolis Museum, c. 410–400 BC). So a three-tier trireme on a vase is stylization and should be drawn from the relief and the Olympias replica.
Red-figure is also the better visual choice. Its frame is mostly glossy black with orange figures, which sets it clearly apart from chapter 2's warm, ochre torch-lit relief. Black-figure is mostly bare orange clay and would sit too close to that palette. A black ground also hands off naturally to the planned transition: the black glaze floods the frame and becomes chapter 4's night sea. The incoming transition (石面卷成陶器曲面，色调转为陶红) still works.
Single shot (locked camera, slowly rotating vase): good and clearly one continuous take. Risks and fixes: (a) use a stamnos or column-krater with a wide frieze. Rotate only about 30–40° over 10 s, so the ship moves from one side to centre and is clearly visible while the card is up. Keep the frieze in the upper-middle of frame so the subtitle area stays clean. (b) Change the oar animation from eighth notes to one stroke every two beats, which matches sprint rates and reads as powerful rather than jittery. (c) Keep the glaze highlight broad and soft, without sharp specular flicker as the vase turns. (d) Optional beat accent: on the final downbeat the ram meets the frieze border, or the running-wave band swells, starting the transition to the night sea.
