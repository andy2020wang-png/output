# Brief: longship

## Style card
Nordic wood-carving aesthetic in 2.5D: layered wave bands moving at different parallax rates (not a 3D sea), with carved ridge edges; a posterised aurora in 3-4 flat tone bands with ridge-like carved edges and Urnes-style S-curves (interlacing animal-ribbon curves). Palette: indigo, deep teal, aurora green; the ochre/red-striped woollen sail is the only warm accent. No star sprinkle, no bloom, no glossy sea — it must not look like a Shadertoy aurora demo.

## The one shot
A crane move rising from just above a wave crest to reveal the longship under the aurora; the crane finishes by 2.5 s, then a slow drift. The bow (dragon/serpent-head prow, clinker-built hull with overlapping strakes visible as carved lines, row of shields only if historically fine — prefer none at sea, oars or sail) rises and falls with the swell; one soft aurora pulse per bar (every 2.5 s). Timber creak events at beats 4 and 12.

## Text
opener(): 维京长船 placed in the aurora sky; subs() over a calm dark wave band.

## Next transition (for awareness; built later in a separate phase)
Out: a carved wave crest becomes the 海水纹 wave border on the rim of a blue-and-white plate.
Keep the last beat (local 9.375-10 s; hook 14.375-15; outro holds to the end) compositionally stable with that anchor element clearly visible.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Proportions: long, low and narrow. Gokstad is 23.8 × 5.1 m, about 4.7:1. A warship like Skuldelev 2 is 29-30 × 3.8 m, more than 9:1 per the museum. Low freeboard (Skuldelev 2 is only 1.77 m deep amidships), a shallow belly, and a sheer line that sweeps up steeply at both ends. A man of about 1.7 m should look small against the hull.
- Double-ended: bow and stern are almost mirror images, each rising into a tall curved post. Do not give it a square transom stern or a raised aftcastle or forecastle.
- Clinker (lapstrake) planking: overlapping oak strakes running from stem to stern, each step visible, with rows of iron rivet heads (Gokstad has 16 strakes per side). Matte tarred brown-black wood, not glossy varnish. Any painted bands on the upper strakes should be muted.
- Rig: one mast amidships, one yard, one square sail. No triangular or lateen sail, no second mast, no bowsprit or jib, no crow's nest, no ratline ladders (just a few shrouds and a stay). The sail was wool, and its colour is not attested: ochre or undyed wool is safest (Sea Stallion uses ochre-dyed wool). Red and white stripes or checks are a common reconstruction choice; avoid logos or symbols on the sail.
- Steering: a side rudder (steering oar) on the starboard (right) quarter at the stern; 'starboard' comes from 'steer-board'. A ship moving left to right on screen shows its starboard side, so the rudder sits on the near side at the left (stern) end. Moving right to left, it is on the far side. No sternpost rudder, no wheel, no centreline tiller.
- Oars: they pass through round oar holes cut in an upper strake (Gokstad has 16 per side), not over the gunwale in oarlocks. Under full sail the oars are pulled inboard. Show either sail filled with oars in, or sail lowered with oars out, not both straining at once. If oars stroke on the beat, one stroke per bar (2.5 s, about 24 strokes per minute) looks realistic; half-note strokes would be frantic.
- Shields: round wooden shields (about 90 cm) with a central iron boss, hung overlapping on a rack along the gunwale. The Gokstad ship carried 64, painted alternately yellow and black. Shields hung over the oar holes rule out rowing, so show the shield row only when the oars are in.
- Figurehead: no dragon-head prow has survived on an excavated Scandinavian ship. The Oseberg stem ends in a coiled serpent spiral. Animal-head prows are attested by the Bayeux Tapestry and the sagas, and a law recorded in Landnámabók required removing them before land came into sight. If you show a dragon, make it a modest removable carved-wood beast head in Urnes or Ringerike style. No gilding, horns, glowing eyes or fire.
- Crew: a 30 m warship carried 60-70 men (Skuldelev 2 had 60 oars). Show wool tunics and cloaks, with conical or rounded helmets (Gjermundbu style) or bare heads. No horned or winged helmets (an 1876 Wagner-costume myth), no plate armour, no fantasy furs.
- Avoid flags: no Nordic-cross flags (the Dannebrog legend dates to 1219, Norway's flag to 1821), no national flags, no ensigns. Also no deck house or cabin, portholes, metal fittings that read as modern, or a stockless modern anchor.
- Do not draw a knarr by mistake: a knarr is broad and high-sided with only a few oars and an open cargo hold amidships. A longship is slim, with oar holes along almost its whole length.
- Aurora lighting: a dominant green band (557.7 nm), fading to faint red or violet at the upper edge, on a moonless sky. The ship reads as a silhouette with a cool rim light. Aurora is too faint to cast hard shadows. Keep the sea under the subtitle area dark and quiet so the card stays readable.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
The plan (极光夜海, a crane shot rising from a wave crest to reveal a dragon-headed longship under aurora; exit with the green turning to cobalt and the sky to glaze for the blue-and-white porcelain shot) works on rhythm and contrast. It follows terracotta black-figure pottery, is the series' first cool-coloured shot, and the green-to-cobalt hand-off into 青花瓷 is elegant. Risks and fixes: (1) It is the only shot whose style card is a mood rather than period art; the neighbours are relief, black-figure ware, porcelain, portolan chart, Turner-style oil painting and engraving. More seriously, it overlaps with shot 12, a real-time ocean-rendered cinematic night sea, and two photoreal night seas would weaken the finale. Give shot 4 a period art signature and keep it non-photoreal: a limited palette (indigo, teal, aurora green, with the ochre sail as the only warm accent) and Norse interlace (Urnes/Ringerike curves) in the aurora curtains and the wave crests, as if carved in wood, so it reads as 'Norse carving come alive' rather than a sea simulation. An era-correct alternative is Bayeux Tapestry wool embroidery on linen (1070s, animal-head prows and shield rows), but it would repeat the 'pan along an artwork' move of shots 2 and 6. Keep the aurora. (2) Plausibility: aurora is invisible during the bright summer nights at Norway's latitude, but Viking voyages did run into spring and autumn, and the 845 Seine fleet sailed in March, so it is defensible licence. (3) Timing: finish the crane reveal by about beat 4 (2.5 s), as the title fades, then hold with a slow drift for the 7 s card. Sync motion to the bow rising and falling over swells and to soft aurora pulses each bar, not to oar strokes under full sail. (4) Treat the dragon head as described in the visual notes: modest and carved. The card's mention of the Seine and Paris does not clash with an open-sea image, because its first clause (横渡北海) matches the picture.
