# Brief: lineship

## Style card
J. M. W. Turner-like oil painting, built to stay stable in motion: 4-5 parallax layers (sky, far sea + distant ships, middle line of ships, near water, smoke), each painted ONCE in init() with baked, oriented brushstrokes; smoke and muzzle glow animated as fbm masks through a dry-brush threshold; a static canvas weave over everything; impasto height only where luminance > ~0.75 (sun, smoke tops, foam). Golden, hazy light. The nearest 2-3 hulls stay hard-edged with readable gunport rows (two or three gun decks, black-and-ochre bands, square rig).

## The one shot
Elevated three-quarter view with a slow lateral track along a line of battle (ships in single file, bow to stern). Exactly four ships fire their broadsides one after another on beats 9, 10, 11, 12 (5.0, 5.625, 6.25, 6.875 s): muzzle flashes along the gun deck, then billowing smoke that drifts and partly veils the line. No flags with real national insignia (use plain coloured pennants or none).

## Text

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Pick one sub-era and keep to it. The clearest choice is the Napoleonic look of about 1800–1805, the same period as Turner. Do not mix 17th-century gilded Anglo-Dutch ships with Trafalgar-era ones in the same line.
- Show a true line ahead: 4–6 ships on the same heading, each directly in the wake of the one ahead, evenly spaced a few hull lengths apart. A faint enemy line can sit parallel in the haze on the engaged side.
- Every ship fires only from the side facing the enemy, and the whole line fires from the same side. Never fire from both sides at once, and never show bow guns as the main armament.
- Hull: wooden, with clear tumblehome (the sides lean inward above the waterline). Give it 2 or 3 continuous rows of square gunports. A 74-gun two-decker was the most common type; a flagship has three decks (Victory carried 104 guns on three gun decks).
- Port lids are hinged at the top and swing up. The inside of the lid is shown red in period models and paintings. Gun carriages are wooden with four small wheels, and the guns are smoothbore muzzle-loaders run out through the ports.
- Paint for about 1805: a black hull with yellow-ochre bands along each gun deck and black port lids, the 'Nelson chequer' (Victory was repainted from red to black and yellow in the 1800–03 refit). Below the waterline the hull is copper-sheathed in a warm copper or verdigris tone (Victory was coppered in 1780).
- Rig: three masts. Fore and main are fully square-rigged with course, topsail and topgallant. The mizzen carries square topsails plus a gaff spanker. The bowsprit and jib-boom carry triangular jibs. In battle, show 'fighting sail': courses clewed up, topsails and topgallants set.
- Stern: tiers of windows or galleries (an open stern walk on a first rate). Bow: beakhead and a carved figurehead. Steering is by rudder only: no propeller, no funnel.
- Gunfire: a short muzzle flash, then a large cloud of dense white-grey black-powder smoke rolling downwind. The gold comes from the sunset light, not from fire. Solid roundshot does not explode, so show splashes, holed sails and falling spars, never fireballs or explosions on the hull (explosive shell guns came only in the 1820s–40s).
- Crew are tiny at this scale: several hundred per ship (Victory's full complement was about 850). Marines in red coats on the quarterdeck and in the tops, sailors in plain clothing, since there was no rating uniform before 1857. Avoid modern naval uniforms.
- Flags: keep them small and indistinct in the haze. Use stern ensigns and long masthead pennants. At Trafalgar the British fleet flew the White Ensign, and any Union canton after 1801 includes the red St Patrick saltire; French ships wore the tricolour from the 1790s. Avoid the Jolly Roger, modern national flags and modern naval jacks. No readable signal-flag text.
- Weather at Trafalgar was light wind with a heavy swell, and the ships closed at only a few knots, so slow, stately movement is historically right.
- Anachronisms to avoid: funnels or steam, iron or grey hulls, turrets, rifled or breech-loading guns, exploding shells, a 'pirate' look, flags on every mast, and full sails set while ships are badly damaged.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
A Romantic oil-painting style card (Turner-like impasto, golden haze) fits the period and stands apart from its neighbours. Turner painted 'The Battle of Trafalgar' (1822–24, National Maritime Museum, Greenwich) and 'The Fighting Temeraire' (1839, National Gallery). It also contrasts clearly with the parchment portolan chart before it (6) and the hand-coloured Victorian engraving after it (8).
Risks and fixes:
(1) Turner's haze dissolves silhouettes, and the card's whole point is the single-file line with gunport rows. Keep the nearest 2–3 hulls hard-edged and readable, gunports included, and let the painterly blur take over only toward the distance and in the smoke. Note that naval officers criticised Turner's own Trafalgar for factual inaccuracies, so take his light, not his loose drawing.
(2) A 'slow push in' risks ending on a single ship and losing the line. Better: an elevated three-quarter view from off the engaged beam, with the column receding diagonally into depth, then a slow lateral track along the line with only a slight push. Broadsides ripple ship by ship on the beats (one ship per beat), so each beat reads as the next ship firing.
(3) Caption legibility: the bright gold and white smoke must not sit behind the 7-second card. Let smoke drift up and away from the caption band, and keep the lower third on a darker sea.
(4) Muzzle flashes should be soft warm glows, not full-frame white flashes, which would also be a photosensitivity risk on Douyin.
(5) Transition idea in the same spirit as the plan: on the last beat the golden powder smoke greys, and a dark coal-smoke plume drifts in from frame edge. This echoes the Fighting Temeraire, where a paddle steam tug's smoke crosses a ghostly white-and-gold Trafalgar veteran (the National Gallery calls it the age of sail giving way to steam). That plume then hardens into the engraving lines of chapter 8. It is the most fitting historical bridge from sail to steam.
