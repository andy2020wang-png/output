# Brief: carrier

## Style card
WWII US Navy combat-art field painting in gouache/watercolour: full bleed, no outlines, no halftone, no paper border; wet edges, granulation, white-paper reserves for highlights; dusk palette of slate and navy with an orange horizon. No lettering, flags, hull numbers or national insignia on aircraft.

## The one shot
From the stern looking forward along a 1942 carrier's wooden flight deck (straight deck, island on the starboard side) — propeller aircraft (generic dive-bomber/torpedo-plane silhouettes with radial engines) spotted aft. One aircraft makes its deck run and lifts off on beats 5, 9, 13 (2.5, 5.0, 7.5 s); at the end the camera lifts to follow the last plane toward an empty horizon (the enemy fleet is never seen — the card says the fleets never sighted each other). Beats 15-16: hold still, dusk deepens.

## Text
opener(): 航空母舰 in a clean period sans (Inter/Noto Sans SC 700); subs() over the darker sea/deck at the bottom with a wash band.

## Next transition (for awareness; built later in a separate phase)
Out: dusk falls into blue hour, deck lights come on.
Keep the last beat (local 9.375-10 s; hook 14.375-15; outro holds to the end) compositionally stable with that anchor element clearly visible.

## Researcher's accuracy notes (follow unless they conflict with the style card above, which wins)
- Pick one 1942 US fleet carrier, Yorktown-class or Lexington, or an unmarked generic WWII carrier. Do not show a Japanese carrier. No Rising Sun naval ensign (旭日旗) and no hinomaru anywhere: they are a serious sensitivity risk on Douyin and for a Chinese audience.
- Put the island on the STARBOARD (right) side. The flight deck is a straight axial deck on a long open-sided wooden deck, with no angled deck (1950s), no steam catapult track and no jets. In 1942, launches were deck runs: aircraft parked aft roll forward and lift off the bow while the ship steams into the wind.
- A Yorktown-class island is compact, with one large raked funnel built into it. A Lexington silhouette needs its very large separate flat-sided funnel aft of the island. Do not mix the two.
- The deck is wooden planking in a dark blue-grey wartime stain, with only thin edge and centre lines. Coral Sea and Midway photos show no big hull numbers painted on the deck. Avoid modern yellow and white landing-area graphics, deck numbers and LED lighting.
- Paint the hull in a wartime navy-blue or blue-grey scheme with no bright colours. Dazzle belongs to HMS Argus in 1918 and to the previous chapter.
- Aircraft: F4F Wildcat (stubby mid-wing fighter), SBD Dauntless (two-seat dive bomber with perforated split flaps; its wings do not fold) and TBD Devastator (long greenhouse canopy, three crew). All are radial-engine propeller aircraft painted blue-grey on top over light grey. No jets, no Corsairs or Hellcats (they came later), no helicopters.
- Insignia: at Coral Sea in May 1942, US aircraft still had the red centre dot in the white star, plus red-and-white rudder stripes. Both were removed around 12–15 May 1942 because the dot could be mistaken for a hinomaru. Simplest: draw a plain white star in a blue disc with no red, or leave insignia off. Never show anything that reads as a red disc.
- Deck crew wear cloth helmets and jerseys. A launch officer signals each takeoff with a flag; use no whistles in the visual or the sound. Planes taking off from the bow sink slightly, then climb.
- Light: the US strikes on 8 May launched around 09:00–09:25 in morning light. Show sun and scattered cumulus over the US carrier. The Japanese carriers were under rain squalls and heavy cloud, so a dark cloud bank on the far horizon fits.
- Pacing: a realistic launch every beat (0.625 s) is impossible. Launch one aircraft per bar (every 4 beats), giving about 3–4 takeoffs in the 10 s shot, or one every 2 beats as a stylised minimum.
- Strengthen the card in the composition: end the shot with the last aircraft shrinking toward an EMPTY horizon with no enemy ship in view. Do not show bombs or burning ships, which would also clash with the poster tone.

## Researcher's style comments (background)
The 二战时期水粉招贴画 style (flat gouache with an airbrushed sky gradient and a limited palette) fits the era, since it is the look of 1940s aviation and recruitment illustration. It reads differently from #10, Vorticism and dazzle: hard-edged, abstract and mostly black and white. It also differs from #12, the realistic night-time cinematic render. Risks: (a) The 招贴画 style invites slogans, national flags and propaganda imagery. Keep it purely pictorial, with no lettering, flags or insignia that could be read as Japanese. (b) Both #10 and #11 are flat graphic styles, so separate them by palette: warm cream and orange sky with navy and slate blue, versus #10's black, white and saturated stripes. Brush and gouache grain should be visible. (c) The plan's single shot (looking forward from the stern as aircraft launch on the beat) matches 1942 deck-run practice. Space the launches one per bar. Let the camera rise slightly at the end to follow the last plane toward an empty horizon, a visual 'never saw each other' that supports the card. (d) The transitions work. Dazzle stripes straightening into deck lines is a good link; Argus herself wore dazzle in 1918. Dusk to deck lights into #12 is also good. #12 opens on a deck as well, but the switch to night and to a pull-back to aerial keeps the two shots distinct. A possible alternative look is 1940s Kodachrome-style colour illustration, but the gouache poster is the better and more distinct choice.
